1. MAIN ANNOUNCEMENT - DRP Final Presentation Overview and Advice (thread #110)
================================================================================
(Mark Wheelhouse)

Hi everyone,

Next week (on Monday) Carolyn, Ivan and I will give a detailed overview of the
DRP Final Presentation assessment criteria, but I wanted to share some general
information and advice about these presentations now, so that you can start to
plan for them accordingly.

During your DRP Final Presentation slot you will have 20 minutes (strictly
timed) to present your work. During your presentation you should:

introduce your project context and problem
(remember that some DRP Tutors won't have seen your work for a few weeks),

describe and demonstrate your proposed solution,

discuss your digital touchpoint's structure and implementation,

and evaluate your project.

This will be followed by 5 minutes of Q&A where your group will take questions
from the DRP assessment panel and (time permitting) general audience.
We have also reserved 5 minutes per slot to allow for the logistics of getting
everyone set-up in 308/311/145 and sorting out any AV sound/video issues.

We want these presentations to be given live (especially any demonstrations),
but it is fine to pre-record your opening pitch (the first 3-5 mins discussed
below) if you want to have this part flow smoothly. We would like each member
of your group to speak for at least 3-4 minutes during your presentation
(ideally for an equal amount of time).

It is my intention to record each group's DRP Final Project Presentation so
that we may share the best project presentations on the DoC website and also
with next year's cohort. On that note, if you would like to see some of the
best presentations from last year, then I previously uploaded the top 6 to the
Best DRP Final Presentations 2024-2025 folder on Panopto.

Your presentation should cover all of the following topics:

An Introduction to your Project:
Your presentation should start with a roughly 5 minute opening pitch and
mini-demo. This should set the scene for your project by explaining the
motivating problem that you have set out to solve as well as your target users
and overarching "How Might We" statement. It should be clear what Human Centred
Design techniques have been employed in generating your problem statement.

A Detailed Description of your Proposed Solution:
You should give a more detailed explanation of your problem solution,
focussing on the various user journeys that your digital touchpoint supports,
ideally through the experiences of your persona(s). It should be clear how
users will interact with your digital touchpoint and what benefit this will
provide to them. It should also be clear that the Human Centred issues
identified in the problem statement have been addressed.

An overview of your application's structure and implementation:
You should discuss how your digital touchpoint has been built and how the
different components interact with one-another. As part of this you should
explain the languages and programming frameworks that you used to implement
your digital touchpoint. This discussion should also include a system
architecture diagram specific to your project. You do not want to go into
excessive detail about every class or function that you have implemented, but
you should highlight the points of the design that you feel were particularly
challenging or that you are proud of. You should give solid justifications for
each of your choices and compare to alternatives you considered. This
discussion should be suitable for a future development team to understand the
structure of your digital touchpoint and the technologies they must understand
if they were to continue your work.

A demonstration of your digital touchpoint in use:
You should show the audience your digital touchpoint in action, particularly
focusing on the user interactions with the system (i.e. user journeys), again
ideally through the experiences of your persona(s). You should be backing-up
all of the claims that you make during your presentation with suitably chosen
examples in your demonstration.

An Evaluation of your Project:
You should reflect on the outcome of your project, the impact/benefits to your
target users and identify possibilities for future work. Summarise what you
have done, what you have not done, what you have learned from the project and
what you would do differently in the future.

Note that the above list should not be considered as the definitive structure
of your presentation. Feel free to address things in whichever order you feel
leads to the most coherent overall story.
For example, you don't need to have all of your evaluation at the end of the
presentation, but could chose to evaluate/reflect on your choices throughout.

Additional Presentation Advice:

When presenting in HXLY 145/308/311, remember that you will have two screens
(though only one external device can be connected) at your disposal. You might
want to think about sharing one of your demo or presentation from the ICT PC
and using a laptop for the other so that you can make efficient use of the AV
set-up.

Clarify in the first slide of your presentation your group number and the title
of your project. This is important and helpful to the DRP Assessment Team -
please don't forget it.

Don't spend more than 5 minutes (already a generous upper bound) on the problem
description. Narrow down your initial problem statement to a couple of minutes
and then start with the fireworks and show a bit of your product. If you go on
too long, you risk loosing the attention of the audience. You can always
revisit the problem/solution in more detail later in the talk.

You probably want to avoid presenting on any generic project management details
such as: group structure, design processes or the use of collaboration tools.
These aspects of the project will either have been enforced by us, or will be
roughly the same for all groups, so there is little interest gained by
presenting them.

Make sure that you demo a working version of your digital touchpoint, even if
this means using an older version. It is much better to say that the current
version of your digital touchpoint does not have some functionality, than to
have it break during the demo.

Don't describe how the user journey changes with your app before demoing. Just
demo it and let the audience see (i.e. show don't tell).

Do not attempt to debug or write code during your presentation. This is not an
efficient use of your time.

You should ideally merge your demonstration and your presentation. Show your
application in action and explain what is happening as it happens. You will
probably want to practice swapping between your slides and your demo. It is
also a good idea to demo something early in the presentation as part of your
introduction.

You may find it helpful to prepare a list of points to cover to keep you on
track during the presentation, but try to avoid reading from a script as this
tends to lead to poor presentation style. It is actually a good idea not to
learn your script by heart, as that's a single point of failure, and who better
than a developer knows how bad that is. Try rehearsing enough to be confident
with multiple alternatives of your scripts. In this way, the chances you will
find yourselves in no-man's land (script-wise) are minimised.

Try to avoid overcrowding your slides. A slide full of text is not very
interesting and distracts the audience from what you are saying. Try using
pictures, diagrams and graphs to convey information where possible. Remember
that adage "Show, Don't Tell".

Watch out for text/background contrast issues. e.g. don't use yellow text on
light grey background, or blue text on black background. This makes it very
difficult (impossible at times) to read anything off your slides. If you are in
doubt, try your slides in presentation mode and ask someone else to judge the
clarity of your slides (ideally in HXLY 145/308/311).

Try to avoid including code snippets in your presentation. A slide full of code
will probably be hard to understand out of context and distracts the audience
from what you are saying. If you really want to highlight an important point,
and are going to explain it in detail, then be succinct with your examples.

Do not just read from the slides. What you are saying should explain what the
audience are seeing, not just repeat it.

Don't speak about "personas" explicitly: personas are abstractions. Saying
"This is Bob, our persona" is like being an actor and walking on stage during
the first act of Romeo and Juliet saying "I am Romeo Montague, a fictional
character". The classic "Meet Bob. Bob is a builder..." works just fine.

Similarly for demos, don't call them that explicitly. Whenever the word "demo"
appears in solitude on a slide, the flow of the presentation is broken. You
should always aim at using the app as you talk, blending the demonstration
seamlessly into the rest of the script.

Consider slide animations with care. If your presentation is overly dynamic it
may disorientate or confuse your audience (Prezi presentations have actually
been known to trigger motion sickness!). Bullet by bullet reveals can also lead
to audience frustration and can waste a noticeable amount of time.

Be sure to rehearse your presentation to ensure that you do not overrun your
presentation slot. Remember that we will want about 5 minutes to ask questions
about your project (this is part of your presentation slot). The ideal
presentation length should be just over 19 minutes. You will need to practice
swapping between speakers and swapping between your slides and your demo so that
you don't waste time during the presentation.

We recommend that you practice your presentations with other groups, taking it
in turns to present to one-another and offer feedback and advice.

We might ask you questions about material you have not covered in your talk.
Please be aware that we are not trying to trick you. We are just trying to find
out what you have done and help you increase your grade. However, it is better
to admit that you do not know something than to try and make things up on the
spot.

Your digital touchpoint should be robust, no inputs should make it fail. Be
prepared to demonstrate user input questions from the audience.

You might find it helpful to write down the steps for your demo so any group
member can run it.

You are encouraged to attend each other's presentations (you can see the
timetable in the presentation booking spreadsheet) to see what other groups
have achieved. It can often be more educational to see how other groups present
than presenting yourself.

Some Notes on System Architecture Diagrams:

Use a sensible number of boxes to represent your components i.e. not 2 (not
informative enough) nor 20 (they are going to be so small on the screen that
the audience won't even try to make sense out of them);

Provide a key to read your arrows:

Do they represent data flow or information flow?

Are they all to be interpreted in the same way?

Anonymous arrows are meaningless to the audience;

Use meaningful names: "component 1" means absolutely nothing to any member of
the audience. Something like "Redis server" provides all the necessary
information.

Submission of Presentation Slides:

You should submit a PDF copy of the final version of your presentation slides
(final_presentation_slides.pdf) to Scientia by 7pm on Friday 19th June.
This is only needed for our assessment records and to provide a clear audit
trail for your performance in this project should any of the external examiners
want to review our marking practices. Your presentation mark won't be affected
by this submission.

2. HOW THE FINAL PRESENTATION IS MARKED (threads #130, #137, #141)
================================================================================

GENERAL ASSESSMENT APPROACH (thread #130, Mark Wheelhouse)
All assessment criteria use a 0-3 scale:
  0 - missing (even after the Q&A session)
  1 - poor / incomplete discussion
  2 - good / expected discussion
  3 - excellent / perfect discussion
Most groups giving a solid presentation will receive mostly 2s, with 3s and 1s
reserved for notably strong or weak elements. No criteria are tick-box marked -
all are judgements of HOW WELL something was done. For example, the timing mark
is not solely about finishing within the 18-21 minute window, but also about
avoiding rushing or padding, both of which the panel will notice.

NO ONE-SIZE EXAMPLE OF A 1/2/3 (thread #137, Mark Wheelhouse)
There is no one-size-fits-all example of what a 1, 2 or 3 looks like for a
criterion (e.g. a "perfect" user testing iteration). Everything depends on each
group's specific problem context, user journeys, and solution.

MARK SCHEME CLARIFICATIONS (thread #141, Mark Wheelhouse)
- Guidance on presenting development iterations: see the user-journey advice
  (thread #136 / section 4 below).
- The "Technical Quality" section of the Assessment Information document (see
  thread #24) breaks down into 5 separate criteria that clarify what needs to
  be presented.
- The "Clear Problem Statement" criterion covers the project context and the
  "How Might We" / opportunity statement too (see thread #134, section 4).

3. DEMOING THE APP (thread #131)
================================================================================
(Mark Wheelhouse)

- The app must be shown running LIVE ON A REAL PHONE for at least part of the
  presentation. Connect via USB-C or use the visualiser available in HXLY
  145/308/311 (setup assistance is available at the start of your slot).
- For geo-location / geofencing features specifically, spoofing the location in
  an emulator OR showing a short pre-recorded video is acceptable - provided the
  app is still seen running live on a phone elsewhere in the presentation.
- Groups have complete freedom in how they present, but authenticity is key:
  showing the app only in an emulator risks not earning full "Stable Product"
  marks.

4. STRUCTURING THE STORY: USER JOURNEYS & PERSONAS (threads #135, #134, #136, #121)
================================================================================

USER-JOURNEY ORDER (thread #135, Mark Wheelhouse)
Present in user-journey order, not in the order you designed/built things -
presenting interactions out of sequence makes the narrative confusing
(analogous to explaining a compiler's back-end before the front-end). You do
not need to cover every design detail; instead repeat this structure 2-3 times
to cover your main stakeholder journeys:
  1. Introduce a user journey involving your service
  2. Demo the relevant user interaction(s)
  3. Discuss how you designed/developed/implemented that interaction (including
     some iteration, but not every step)
You are showing the FINISHED project, not the exact path you took to build it.
Prefer the live app over screenshots ("show, don't tell").

PROBLEM STATEMENT vs OPPORTUNITY STATEMENT (thread #134, Mark Wheelhouse)
The assessment criteria are general categories, not a checklist. The "Clear
Problem Statement" criterion covers how well the project context and problem are
defined, which SHOULD include the "How Might We" opportunity statement.

TWO PERSONAS WITH SIMILAR JOURNEYS (thread #136, Mark Wheelhouse)
Introduce both personas early (so no character appears "out of nowhere" later).
If their initial journeys are very similar, present both, then note the shared
journey and show just one as the example rather than walking through each.

ACTORS PORTRAYING A PERSONA (thread #121, Mark Wheelhouse)
Yes, having an actor portray your persona in a video for the final presentation
is allowed.

5. WHAT TO SHOWCASE / POST-MILESTONE CHANGES (threads #139, #106, #112)
================================================================================

UI REDESIGN AFTER MILESTONE 4 (thread #139, Jamie Willis / Mark Wheelhouse)
Making changes after Milestone 4 in response to user or tutor feedback is no
problem (colour schemes, layout, look and feel). The final presentation should
showcase the project as a "finished product"; only a SMALL amount of discussion
about the design/development process is expected.

WEEK 4 MILESTONE vs FINAL PRESENTATION (thread #106, Mark Wheelhouse)
- The Week 4 session is a milestone review: 10-15 minutes, more discursive,
  assessed on PROCESS rather than outcomes.
- The final presentation is a 20-minute session where you present the final
  state of your project for the full 20 minutes WITHOUT interruption.

REUSING THE MILESTONE 1 PITCH (thread #112, Mark Wheelhouse)
You may reuse parts of your Milestone 1 Elevator Pitch in the final
presentation opening, but most groups now understand their problem context far
more deeply than three weeks ago, so significant reworking of any pre-recorded
content is probably warranted. There is NO requirement to include any
pre-recorded content at all.

7. GENERAL PRESENTATION TIPS (thread #34, originally for the M1 pitch)
================================================================================
(Mark Wheelhouse - these slide/delivery tips carry over to the final.)

- Slides: avoid overcrowding with text; check text-background contrast in
  presentation mode and have someone else judge readability.
- Delivery: practise beforehand in the delivery format; don't hold scripts or
  phones; don't memorise a single fixed script - rehearse multiple alternatives
  instead; don't rely on a teammate to advance your slides (it rarely works).
- HCD: don't refer to personas explicitly as "personas" - introduce them
  naturally (e.g. "Meet Bob. Bob is a builder...").
